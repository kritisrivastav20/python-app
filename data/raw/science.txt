# SCIENCE – CLASS 5 LEARNING MATERIAL

## 1. Living and Non-Living Things

Everything around us can be divided into living and non-living things. Humans, animals, plants, birds, insects, and microorganisms are living things. Rocks, water, soil, buildings, tables, books, and machines are non-living things.

Living things have certain characteristics that separate them from non-living things. Living organisms need food and water. They grow, breathe, reproduce, respond to their surroundings, and eventually die.

Plants are also living organisms. Although plants cannot walk from one place to another like animals, they grow and respond to changes in their environment. For example, the stem of a plant usually grows toward light.

Animals need food to obtain energy. Different animals eat different types of food. Herbivores such as cows and deer mainly eat plants. Carnivores such as lions and tigers eat other animals. Omnivores such as humans and bears can eat both plants and animals.

Non-living things do not perform life processes on their own. A car can move, but it cannot move without fuel and a driver or another control system. A car does not grow, reproduce, or need food like a living organism.

Understanding living organisms helps us learn about the great variety of life found on Earth.

## 2. Plants and Their Parts

Plants are extremely important for life on Earth. They provide food, oxygen, medicines, wood, fibres, and shelter. Plants also provide habitats for many animals.

A typical plant has roots, a stem, leaves, flowers, fruits, and seeds.

Roots usually grow below the ground. They hold the plant firmly in the soil and absorb water and minerals. Some plants have taproots, while others have fibrous roots.

A taproot consists of one main root with smaller roots growing from it. Carrot and radish plants are examples of plants with prominent taproots.

Fibrous roots consist of many thin roots growing from the base of the stem. Grass, rice, and wheat are examples of plants with fibrous root systems.

The stem supports the plant. It carries water and minerals from the roots to other parts of the plant. It also helps transport food made by the leaves.

Leaves are sometimes called the food factories of plants because they produce food through photosynthesis.

Flowers are reproductive structures of many plants. After pollination and fertilization, parts of a flower can develop into fruits containing seeds.

Seeds can grow into new plants when they receive suitable conditions.

## 3. Photosynthesis

Plants need food just like animals, but green plants can make their own food.

The process through which green plants make food is called photosynthesis.

Plants require sunlight, water, carbon dioxide, and chlorophyll for photosynthesis.

Roots absorb water from the soil. The water travels through the stem and reaches the leaves.

Leaves take carbon dioxide from the air through tiny openings called stomata.

Leaves contain a green pigment called chlorophyll. Chlorophyll helps the plant capture light energy from the Sun.

Using light energy, plants produce sugars from carbon dioxide and water. Oxygen is released during the process.

A simplified description is:

Carbon dioxide + Water + Light energy → Sugar + Oxygen

Photosynthesis is extremely important because it produces food that supports much of life on Earth.

Plants are eaten by herbivores. Herbivores may then be eaten by carnivores. Therefore, energy originally captured by plants can pass through a food chain.

Photosynthesis also releases oxygen into the atmosphere. Humans and many other organisms depend on oxygen for respiration.

## 4. Seeds and Germination

A seed contains a young developing plant. Under suitable conditions, a seed can develop into a new plant.

The process by which a seed begins to grow into a new plant is called germination.

Seeds generally need water, air, and a suitable temperature to germinate.

When a seed receives water, it absorbs the water and swells. The seed coat eventually breaks. A small root begins to grow downward into the soil.

Later, a young shoot grows upward. Small leaves develop and begin receiving sunlight.

Once leaves develop, the young plant can increasingly make its own food through photosynthesis.

Seeds can be dispersed in many ways.

Some seeds are carried by wind. They may be small and light or have structures that help them float through the air.

Some seeds are dispersed by water. Coconut fruits, for example, can float and travel through water.

Animals also disperse seeds. Some fruits are eaten by animals, and their seeds may later be deposited somewhere else.

Some plants produce fruits that burst open when mature and scatter their seeds.

Seed dispersal helps prevent too many new plants from growing in exactly the same place.

## 5. Animals and Their Habitats

A habitat is the natural environment in which an organism lives.

Different animals live in different habitats because they have different requirements.

Forests contain large numbers of plants and animals. Tigers, elephants, monkeys, deer, insects, snakes, and birds can live in forest ecosystems.

Deserts receive very little rainfall. Animals living in deserts have adaptations that help them survive with limited water.

Camels are well adapted to desert environments. Their bodies have features that help them tolerate dry conditions and conserve water.

Polar regions are extremely cold. Animals such as polar bears have thick fur and layers of body fat that help reduce heat loss.

Aquatic animals live mainly in water. Fish have streamlined bodies and usually breathe using gills.

Some animals, such as frogs, can live both on land and in water during different stages or activities.

Birds have several adaptations for flight. Many birds have lightweight skeletons, feathers, wings, and powerful flight muscles.

An adaptation is a characteristic that helps an organism survive and reproduce in its environment.

## 6. Food Chains and Food Webs

Living organisms depend on one another for food.

A food chain shows how food and energy pass from one organism to another.

Consider this example:

Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Eagle

Grass produces food using sunlight. The grasshopper eats the grass. The frog eats the grasshopper. The snake may eat the frog, and an eagle may eat the snake.

Plants are called producers because they can produce their own food through photosynthesis.

Animals cannot make food in the same way. They obtain energy by consuming plants or other organisms and are called consumers.

Herbivores are animals that mainly eat plants.

Carnivores eat other animals.

Omnivores eat both plant and animal material.

Decomposers are also important. Organisms such as many fungi and bacteria break down dead plants and animals. This process returns nutrients to the environment.

In nature, animals often eat several different types of food. Therefore, many food chains connect together to form a food web.

Changes to one population can affect other organisms within a food web.

## 7. The Human Body

The human body contains many organs that work together.

A group of organs that cooperate to perform important functions forms an organ system.

The digestive system breaks food into substances that the body can absorb and use.

Digestion begins in the mouth. Teeth break food into smaller pieces, while saliva mixes with food.

Food travels through the food pipe, or oesophagus, to the stomach.

The stomach mixes food with digestive substances and continues breaking it down.

Digestion and absorption continue in the intestines. Nutrients are absorbed and transported to different parts of the body.

The respiratory system helps us obtain oxygen.

Air enters through the nose or mouth and travels toward the lungs. In the lungs, oxygen from the air can enter the blood, while carbon dioxide can move from the blood into the air to be breathed out.

The circulatory system transports substances around the body.

The heart pumps blood through blood vessels. Blood carries oxygen and nutrients to tissues and carries some waste products away.

The brain, spinal cord, and nerves form important parts of the nervous system. This system helps control and coordinate activities throughout the body.

## 8. Food and Nutrition

Our bodies require nutrients to remain healthy, grow, repair tissues, and obtain energy.

Carbohydrates are an important source of energy. Foods such as rice, bread, potatoes, cereals, and wheat contain carbohydrates.

Proteins are important for growth and tissue repair. Pulses, beans, eggs, milk, fish, meat, and nuts can provide protein.

Fats provide energy and perform several important functions in the body. Oils, butter, nuts, and seeds contain fats.

Vitamins and minerals are required in smaller amounts but are essential for good health.

Fruits and vegetables are useful sources of many vitamins and minerals.

Water is also essential. It helps transport substances, regulate body temperature, and support many chemical processes in the body.

Dietary fibre helps food move through the digestive system.

A balanced diet contains appropriate amounts of different nutrients.

Eating only one type of food cannot usually provide everything our bodies require.

Good eating habits include eating a variety of nutritious foods, drinking sufficient water, and limiting foods containing excessive amounts of sugar, salt, or unhealthy fats.

## 9. Teeth and Dental Health

Teeth help us bite, tear, crush, and grind food.

Humans develop two main sets of teeth during their lives.

The first set is called milk teeth or temporary teeth. These teeth eventually fall out and are replaced by permanent teeth.

Different types of teeth have different functions.

Incisors are the front teeth and are useful for cutting and biting food.

Canines are pointed teeth that help tear food.

Premolars and molars have broader surfaces that help crush and grind food.

Keeping teeth clean is important.

Food particles can remain between teeth after eating. Bacteria in the mouth can use sugars from food and produce acids. These acids can damage tooth enamel and contribute to cavities.

Brushing teeth regularly helps remove food particles and plaque.

Reducing frequent consumption of sugary foods and drinks can also help protect teeth.

Regular dental check-ups can identify problems before they become more serious.

## 10. Matter

Everything that has mass and occupies space is called matter.

The objects around us are made of matter.

Matter commonly exists as solids, liquids, and gases.

A solid has a definite shape and volume. A book, pencil, stone, and ice cube are examples of solids.

A liquid has a definite volume but takes the shape of its container. Water, milk, and cooking oil are liquids.

A gas does not have a fixed shape or fixed volume. It spreads to fill the available space in a container.

Matter can change from one state to another.

When ice is heated, it melts and becomes liquid water.

When water is cooled sufficiently, it freezes and becomes ice.

When liquid water gains enough energy, some of it changes into water vapour. This process is called evaporation.

When water vapour cools and becomes liquid water, the process is called condensation.

These changes are important in many natural processes, including the water cycle.

## 11. The Water Cycle

Water continuously moves between Earth's surface and atmosphere.

This movement is known as the water cycle.

The Sun provides energy that heats water in oceans, lakes, rivers, and other places.

Some liquid water changes into water vapour and enters the atmosphere. This is evaporation.

Plants can also release water vapour from their leaves through a process called transpiration.

As moist air rises and cools, water vapour can condense into tiny droplets. Large groups of these droplets can form clouds.

When droplets or ice crystals in clouds become sufficiently large, water returns to Earth's surface as precipitation.

Rain, snow, sleet, and hail are forms of precipitation.

Water may enter the ground, collect in lakes, flow into rivers, or eventually return to the oceans.

The cycle then continues.

The amount of water on Earth is enormous, but only a fraction is easily available as fresh water for human use.

Therefore, conserving clean water is important.

## 12. Air and the Atmosphere

Earth is surrounded by a layer of gases called the atmosphere.

Air is a mixture of gases.

Nitrogen forms the largest portion of Earth's atmosphere. Oxygen forms another large portion and is essential for the respiration of humans and many other organisms.

Plants use carbon dioxide during photosynthesis.

Air also contains water vapour and small amounts of other gases.

Air occupies space even though we cannot normally see it.

For example, when we blow air into a balloon, the balloon expands because the air occupies space.

Moving air is called wind.

Wind can range from a gentle breeze to extremely strong winds during storms.

Air pollution occurs when harmful substances enter the atmosphere.

Smoke from vehicles, industries, fires, and other sources can contribute to air pollution.

Reducing pollution and using cleaner sources of energy can help protect air quality.

## 13. Force, Work and Energy

A force is a push or a pull.

Forces can change the motion, direction, or shape of an object.

When we push a door, we apply force.

When we pull a drawer open, we also apply force.

Gravity is a force that attracts objects with mass toward one another. Near Earth's surface, gravity causes unsupported objects to fall toward the ground.

Friction is a force that opposes motion between surfaces in contact.

Friction can be useful. It allows us to walk without continuously slipping.

However, friction can also produce heat and cause surfaces to wear down.

Energy is the ability to cause change or do work.

Energy exists in different forms.

Light energy comes from sources such as the Sun and lamps.

Thermal energy is associated with the motion of particles and relates to temperature and heat transfer.

Electrical energy powers many machines and appliances.

Chemical energy is stored in substances such as food, fuels, and batteries.

Energy can change from one form to another.

## 14. Simple Machines

Machines help us perform tasks.

A simple machine can make work easier by changing the size or direction of a force.

Six commonly discussed simple machines are the lever, pulley, wheel and axle, inclined plane, wedge, and screw.

A lever consists of a rigid object that moves around a fixed point called a fulcrum.

A seesaw is an example of a lever.

A pulley consists of a wheel with a rope or similar material running around it. Pulley systems can help lift heavy objects.

An inclined plane is a sloping surface. A ramp is an example.

Instead of lifting a heavy object straight upward, we can sometimes move it along a ramp using a smaller force over a greater distance.

A wedge can be used for splitting or cutting.

A screw is essentially an inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder.

A wheel and axle can make it easier to move or rotate objects.

Many complex machines combine several simple machines.

## 15. Light and Shadows

Light allows us to see objects.

The Sun is Earth's most important natural source of light.

Objects that produce their own light are called luminous objects. The Sun, stars, flames, and switched-on lamps are examples.

Objects that do not produce their own visible light are non-luminous. We usually see them because light reflects from their surfaces and enters our eyes.

Materials can interact with light in different ways.

Transparent materials allow much of the light to pass through them. Clear glass is an example.

Translucent materials allow some light through but scatter it.

Opaque materials block most visible light from passing directly through them.

A shadow forms when an opaque object blocks light.

The size and direction of a shadow depend on the positions of the light source and the object.

Shadows change during the day because Earth's rotation changes the apparent position of the Sun in the sky.

## 16. Sound

Sound is produced by vibrations.

When an object vibrates, it can cause nearby particles to vibrate. These vibrations can travel through a medium and may eventually reach our ears.

A vibrating guitar string produces sound.

When we speak, structures in the voice box vibrate.

Sound can travel through solids, liquids, and gases.

Sound cannot travel through a perfect vacuum because there are no particles available to transmit the vibrations.

Different sounds can have different pitches and loudness.

Pitch is related to the frequency of vibrations.

Rapid vibrations generally produce higher-pitched sounds than slower vibrations.

Very loud sounds can damage hearing. Protecting our ears from excessive noise is therefore important.

Noise pollution can affect humans and other animals. Reducing unnecessary loud sounds can make environments healthier and more comfortable.

## 17. Earth and Its Structure

Earth is the planet on which we live.

Earth has several major internal layers.

The crust is Earth's thin outermost solid layer.

Below the crust lies the mantle, which makes up a large portion of Earth's interior.

Deeper inside Earth is the core. Scientists divide the core into an outer core and an inner core.

Earth's surface contains continents and oceans.

Large portions of the surface are covered by water.

Earth's outer shell is divided into large sections called tectonic plates. These plates move very slowly.

Their movements can contribute to the formation of mountains, earthquakes, and volcanic activity.

Rocks are naturally occurring solid materials composed of minerals or mineral-like substances.

Rocks can be classified into three major groups: igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic.

Igneous rocks form when molten rock cools and solidifies.

Sedimentary rocks often form when sediments accumulate and become compacted or cemented.

Metamorphic rocks form when existing rocks are changed by heat, pressure, or other geological processes.

## 18. The Solar System

Our solar system consists of the Sun and objects that orbit it.

The Sun is a star located at the centre of the solar system.

Eight planets orbit the Sun.

In order from the Sun, they are:

Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune.

Mercury is the closest planet to the Sun.

Venus has a thick atmosphere and an extremely hot surface.

Earth has liquid water on its surface and supports a great variety of known life.

Mars is sometimes called the Red Planet because iron-containing minerals on its surface give it a reddish appearance.

Jupiter is the largest planet in the solar system.

Saturn is famous for its extensive ring system.

Uranus and Neptune are distant planets with very cold upper atmospheres.

Planets do not produce visible light in the same way stars do. We can see planets because they reflect light from the Sun.

## 19. The Sun, Earth and Moon

Earth rotates on an imaginary axis.

One complete rotation takes approximately twenty-four hours and gives us the cycle of day and night.

The side of Earth facing the Sun experiences daylight, while the side facing away experiences night.

Earth also revolves around the Sun.

One complete revolution takes approximately 365 days and about six hours.

Earth's axis is tilted relative to its orbit around the Sun. This tilt, combined with Earth's revolution, produces seasonal changes in many regions.

The Moon is Earth's natural satellite.

The Moon revolves around Earth and also rotates.

The Moon does not produce its own visible light. It appears bright because sunlight reflects from its surface.

As the Moon moves around Earth, we see different portions of its illuminated half. These changing appearances are called the phases of the Moon.

A lunar month includes phases such as the new moon, crescent moon, quarter moon, gibbous moon, and full moon.

## 20. Weather and Climate

Weather describes atmospheric conditions over a relatively short period.

Weather can include temperature, rainfall, wind, humidity, cloud cover, and other conditions.

Weather can change quickly.

A morning may be sunny while the afternoon may be cloudy and rainy.

Climate describes the typical weather patterns of a region over much longer periods.

Different regions have different climates.

Some places are hot and dry, while others may be cool and wet.

Weather forecasts use observations and computer models to predict future atmospheric conditions.

Meteorologists study weather and the atmosphere.

Instruments help scientists measure weather conditions.

A thermometer measures temperature.

A rain gauge measures rainfall.

An anemometer measures wind speed.

Weather satellites provide information about clouds, storms, temperature patterns, and other atmospheric conditions.

## 21. Natural Resources

Natural resources are materials and substances obtained from nature that humans and other organisms use.

Water, air, soil, sunlight, forests, minerals, and fossil fuels are examples.

Some natural resources are renewable.

Renewable resources can be naturally replenished over relatively short periods when managed appropriately.

Sunlight and wind are examples of renewable energy resources.

Some resources are non-renewable because they form extremely slowly compared with the rate at which humans use them.

Coal, petroleum, and natural gas are examples of fossil fuels.

Fossil fuels formed from ancient organic material over millions of years.

Burning fossil fuels releases energy, but it also releases carbon dioxide and other pollutants.

Using resources carefully helps ensure they remain available for future generations.

## 22. Environment and Pollution

The environment includes living and non-living surroundings.

Humans depend on the environment for food, water, air, shelter, and natural resources.

Pollution occurs when harmful substances or forms of energy contaminate the environment.

Air pollution affects the atmosphere.

Water pollution can affect rivers, lakes, groundwater, and oceans.

Land pollution can occur when waste and harmful chemicals are improperly disposed of.

Plastic waste is a major environmental problem because many plastics remain in the environment for long periods.

Reducing waste can help protect ecosystems.

The three Rs are:

Reduce, Reuse, and Recycle.

Reduce means using fewer unnecessary resources.

Reuse means using objects again rather than immediately throwing them away.

Recycle means processing certain waste materials so that they can be used to manufacture new products.

Protecting forests, conserving water, reducing pollution, and managing waste responsibly can help protect the environment.

## 23. Forests and Wildlife

Forests are ecosystems containing trees, other plants, animals, fungi, microorganisms, soil, water, and air.

Forests provide habitats for enormous numbers of species.

They also influence the water cycle, store carbon, protect soil, and provide resources for humans.

Deforestation is the removal of forests on a large scale.

Forests may be cleared for agriculture, roads, buildings, mining, or other activities.

Excessive deforestation can destroy habitats and reduce biodiversity.

Wildlife conservation involves protecting wild animals, plants, and their habitats.

National parks, wildlife sanctuaries, and other protected areas can help conserve ecosystems.

Protecting a species often requires protecting its entire habitat.

Every organism has a role within its ecosystem. Removing or greatly reducing one species can sometimes affect many others.

## 24. Adaptation and Survival

Living organisms have characteristics that help them survive in particular environments.

These characteristics are called adaptations.

Fish generally have streamlined bodies that help them move through water. Most fish use gills to obtain oxygen from water.

Many birds have beaks adapted to the food they eat.

Birds that eat seeds may have strong beaks for cracking them.

Birds that catch prey may have sharp, curved beaks.

Some animals use camouflage. Their colours or patterns help them blend with their surroundings.

A stick insect resembles a small branch, making it harder for some predators to notice.

Plants also have adaptations.

Cacti living in dry environments have features that help reduce water loss and store water.

Aquatic plants may have structures that help them float or exchange gases efficiently near the water surface.

Adaptations develop across generations and help explain why organisms living in different environments can look and behave differently.

## 25. Scientific Investigation

Science is not simply a collection of facts.

Science is also a method of investigating questions about the natural world.

Scientists begin by making observations.

An observation may lead to a question.

A scientist can then propose a possible explanation called a hypothesis.

Experiments can be designed to test hypotheses.

A fair experiment changes one important factor while keeping other relevant conditions as similar as possible.

Scientists collect data during experiments.

Data can include measurements, descriptions, counts, photographs, or other observations.

Scientists analyze their results and determine whether the evidence supports their hypothesis.

Sometimes an experiment does not produce the expected result. This does not necessarily make the experiment useless. Unexpected results can reveal problems with an idea or lead to new questions.

Scientific experiments should be repeatable whenever possible.

Other scientists can repeat experiments to determine whether they obtain similar results.

Scientific knowledge can change when researchers discover better evidence.

Curiosity, careful observation, logical thinking, accurate measurement, and willingness to reconsider explanations are all important parts of science.

Science helps us understand everything from tiny cells and atoms to planets, stars, ecosystems, oceans, weather, and the enormous universe around us.
